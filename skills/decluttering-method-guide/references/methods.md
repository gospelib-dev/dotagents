# The methods, one by one

### 3.1 KonMari (Marie Kondo)
Procedure [popular claim, procedure confirmed by encyclopedia summaries]: one concentrated
effort, by category not room, order clothes, books, papers, komono (misc), sentimental. Hold
each item, keep what sparks joy, thank and release the rest, then give every kept item a fixed
home. Vertical folding is part of the storage step.
Best fit: motivated adult, strong gut feelings, lots of clothes/books, wants one dramatic reset.
Failure modes: needs a large time block and gathering space; joy is a weak test for
utilitarian items (tools, cords, documents); couples/families disagree on "joy"; children's
and partner's items are not yours to decide; maintenance was the weakest part in a small
interview study.
Evidence: a 2017 qualitative study of 11 women found discarding easier than keeping order;
no study tests outcomes [research, tiny, qualitative]. Treat as unproven but harmless.
Criticism: oversimplifies; cultural fit questioned; Kondo herself relaxed her rigor after
having more children [popular claim, from encyclopedia].

### 3.2 Dana K. White (container concept, no-mess decluttering)
Procedure [professional practice/popular]:
- Remove visible trash first. Make the decision on each item immediately; avoid sorting
  into temporary piles that become new clutter if you stop. Progress and only progress.
- A container (drawer, shelf, bin, closet, room) is a limit. Gather like items, count them,
  keep your favorites until the container is full; the overflow goes.
- Declutter before buying organizers; if storage is needed, trial with a box you already own.
- Visibility: stuff you cannot see or reach is clutter in practice. Her test is "where would
  I look for it?" - store each item where you would search first. [I could not verify this
  phrase in an opened source; treat as paraphrase of her visibility idea.]
- Priorities: remove clutter, keep daily essentials like dishes under control, deep clean last.
- Kids: give each child a container per category (e.g., one bin of a toy type). What fits
  stays; overflow means the child chooses. She also advises removing "clutter collector"
  toy boxes and notes parents sometimes do purges while kids are away, with a caveat that
  involving kids is often necessary and upsetting.
Best fit: overwhelmed people, perfectionists, people who stall; families (kids' toys, linens,
mugs, craft supplies); maintenance (capacity is a permanent rule).
Failure modes: containers need flexible sizing for fluctuating or odd-shaped items; "fit the
shelf" can push stuff into a bigger container; sentimental and paper need extra rules.
Evidence: practitioner method only [popular claim]; no outcome studies found.

### 3.3 Julie Morgenstern: theme + SPACE (+ zoning)
Procedure [professional practice; summarised via a magazine article]: define what each
room (and each zone in it) is for. Then SPACE: Sort (does it fit the theme?), Purge, Assign
a convenient consistent home (keep habits that already work), Containerize by how things are
used, Equalize (check it works, tweak regularly). Start in the room used most.
Zones: split multipurpose rooms (cooking, dishes, baking), give each a mini-theme, run SPACE.
Best fit: spaces that are cluttered but function is wrong; shared offices/kitchens; people
who need layout logic.
Failure modes: slow if started with large volume; containerizing too early; needs buy-in on
the theme. The "price per square foot" idea (credited to Andy Rooney in the same article)
is a useful prompt for stored but unused bulk.
Evidence: practitioner method; the BCPO exam outline covers consolidate-sort-categorize,
eliminate excess, containerize, optimize space, and maintain systems, i.e. SPACE-like
steps are the profession's baseline [professional practice].

### 3.4 Peter Walsh: vision first
Procedure [popular claim, from interview/excerpt snippets, not full text]: set a vision for
the room, agree on it with everyone who shares it, decide what supports the vision and what
does not, purge what does not. Keep the vision written down and decide only against it.
Best fit: couples/families who disagree; rooms that "should" feel a certain way.
Failure modes: visions can be aspirational fantasies (a hobby room for a hobby never done);
pair with a use test (SKILL.md section 3).

### 3.5 Swedish death cleaning (dostadning)
Procedure [popular claim, summaries]: begin early, treat it as continuous. Start with
bulk storage (basement, attic) and entry closets, then least-sentimental categories
(clothes, books), photographs and sentimental items last; roughly a week per remaining
room. Tell relatives, invite them to take things, label items with a recipient, give
donations away. Do sentimental items with someone, not alone.
Best fit: older adults, inheriting households, anyone with big storage backlog.
Failure modes: label morbid for some; younger families rarely need the legacy framing.
Criticism: critics call it morbid; historians frame the modern version as a response to
accumulation by consumer society.
Useful transfer: "give with a story to a named person" beats donating anonymously for
heirlooms; it also reduces guilt.

### 3.6 FlyLady (zones, routines, 15 minutes)
Procedure [popular claim]: small daily "baby steps"; shiny sink first as a win; five house
zones rotated across the weeks, one mission per day; morning/evening routines; 15-minute
timer sessions; weekly 1-hour home blessing split into ten-minute tasks; "good enough is
good enough" against perfectionism. Decluttering is framed as removing items, not organizing.
Best fit: low-energy, ADHD-leaning, perfectionist households; maintenance.
Failure modes: long setup of routines; guilt if streaks break; slow for big backlogs.
Evidence: no trials; the habit research in `time-evidence-and-limits.md` supports the logic of tiny repeated cues.

### 3.7 The Minimalists: 90/90, 20/20, packing party, 30-day game
- 90/90: used it in the last 90 days? will you in the next 90? If both no, release. The
  window is personal (120 days, six months) [popular claim].
- 20/20: if you truly needed a released item, could you replace it for under $20 within 20
  minutes of here? Authors report replacing fewer than five items between them and call
  it a rule of thumb [popular claim]. Critique: ignores sentiment, rebuy cost and waste;
  do not use for heirlooms, tools you use yearly, or things hard to replace locally.
- Packing party: pack nearly everything as if moving; unpack only as needed; consider
  releasing what stays boxed after about three weeks [popular claim; secondary source].
  Needs a partner and logistic room; a family with kids rarely can.
- Minimalism Game: partner challenge: one item day 1, two day 2, up to 30 by day 30, all
  out of the house by midnight [popular claim]. Good gamified motivation for willing
  adults; produces poor quality choices late in the month if unplanned.
- The Minimalists say decluttering is only the first step; the "why" matters or the
  space refills.

### 3.8 Four-Box (keep / donate-sell / trash / relocate)
Procedure [popular claim]: label four boxes; handle each item once; trash and relocate
get cleared as you go. Variants add a "maybe." Best fit: first-time, time-limited, kids
involved, one closet or drawer at a time.
Failure modes: "relocate" box becomes a house-wide shuffle; maybe pile becomes permanent;
does not change buying habits. OHIO ("only handle it once") is the same idea from the
hoarding-organizer literature: avoid churning, the repeated pick-up and put-down without
deciding.

### 3.9 One-in-one-out (OIOO)
Procedure: each new item in means one out. Best fit: maintenance of a decluttered closet,
toy shelf, bookshelf. Failure modes: does not shrink existing volume (some use one-in-two-out);
does not fix the buying itself; fails if the "out" is postponed. [popular claim]

### 3.10 The Home Edit (categorize, edit, contain, label, color-sort) 
I could not open a source describing its method; only the company history (founded 2015,
widely branded) was verified. From general professional knowledge [professional practice,
unverified here]: it is an aesthetics-and-containment system. Best used after a purge,
for visibility and re-finding; fails if applied before cutting volume, and labels/containers
bought first become new clutter (the Dana White objection).

### 3.11 Danshari (Hideko Yamashita)
Refuse, dispose, separate from attachment to unneeded things (encyclopedia summary only).
Fit: people who accumulate through acquisition; pair with an intake rule.
