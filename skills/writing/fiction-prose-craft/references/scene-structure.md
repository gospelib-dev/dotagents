# Scene structure: scene and sequel, turns, beats

## Swain's units (Techniques of the Selling Writer, 1965, ch. 4)
- **Scene** = goal → conflict → disaster. A unit of conflict: an effort to reach a goal despite
  opposition. Disasters are "No, and…" or "Yes, but…" outcomes. They need not be catastrophic, only
  consequential (Fawkes summary of Swain).
- **Sequel** = reaction → dilemma → decision. A unit of transition that turns the disaster into a new goal,
  compresses time and controls tempo (Swain via Wikipedia "Scene and sequel"; Fawkes). Reaction is
  emotional. Dilemma is logical. Decision gives the next scene its goal.
- Ingermanson renames these "proactive" and "reactive" scenes (Writing Fiction for Dummies; blog "Passive
  Sequels in Fiction Writing"). Scenes lead to sequels, and sequels lead to scenes.
- **Butcher's four sequel jobs** (LiveJournal "SEQUELS"): (1) emotional reaction, (2) review facts and
  logic, (3) ponder likely outcomes, (4) choice, which is the new goal. His tuning advice: a character who
  reads as flighty needs more reason in their sequels; a dry one needs more emotion; unclear motivation
  means the sequels need work.
- Sequels are where summary is useful (Fawkes on Swain).

## McKee's turn (Story, 1997, "The Structure Spectrum" and scene analysis)
- A scene is "an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the
  value-charged condition of a character's life".
- **Diagnostic:** note the value and its charge (+/−) at the top of the scene and at the close. If they are
  the same, the scene is a non-event. "No scene that doesn't turn." If exposition is a scene's only
  justification, cut the scene and weave its information in elsewhere.
- Beats are exchanges of action and reaction. The turning point is the beat where the value flips.
- Changing location is not a new scene. A scene ends when the value changes.

## Applying it to a chapter
1. Split the chapter at its scene breaks. For each part, write a line of the form G/C/D or R/D/D.
2. Mark the value and its charge at each end.
3. Look for **two beats that do the same job**: restated commitment and restated finality are the usual sag.
4. Check that sequels are not passive: the decision must be a choice, not drift (Ingermanson).
