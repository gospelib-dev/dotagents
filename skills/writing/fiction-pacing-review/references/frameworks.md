# Structural frameworks and scene diagnostics

Use frameworks as alternate lenses. A chapter may pass one and fail another; the prose’s intended effect decides.

## 1. Causal wholeness

Aristotle defines a whole action as having beginning, middle, and end, and distinguishes causal “because of” sequence from mere succession. He also requires reversal/recognition to arise from the plot’s internal structure [S1].

**Test:**

- Beginning: what condition creates the chapter’s live problem?
- Middle: what choices and obstacles transform it?
- End: what is resolved, reversed, or newly made inevitable?
- Delete each beat mentally. If nothing downstream changes, it may be detachable episode.

This is a causal test, not a demand for symmetry.

## 2. Three-act lens

Syd Field’s official paradigm names Act I setup, Act II confrontation, and Act III resolution, with plot points turning action into a new direction [S3]. Applied to a novel chapter:

| Function | Chapter question |
|---|---|
| Setup | What changed now, and what does the viewpoint character want? |
| Confrontation | What resists, complicates, or reframes the attempt? |
| Resolution | What outcome changes the next chapter’s available choices? |

Do not import Field’s screenplay page numbers into a novel. Use act labels only when they reveal a missing turn or overlong setup.


## 3. Freytag lens

Freytag’s original dramatic model describes a five-part dramatic action around a climax; it was built for drama, not as a mandatory novel beat sheet [S2]. Use it to ask whether pressure rises, turns, and releases with proportionate consequence. Do not force every chapter into a pyramid.

## 4. Promise–progress–payoff

Sanderson’s SF/F lecture defines promise as reader expectation, progress as meaningful movement, and payoff as a fulfilling answer; progress can be plot, information, relationship, or internal change [S7].

**Ledger:**

| Promise | Progress signposts | Payoff or deliberate carry |
|---|---|---|
| explicit question | decisions / discoveries / costs | answer, reversal, or stronger question |
| emotional need | trust shift / confession / refusal | changed relationship |
| world mystery | sensory clue / rule under pressure | usable knowledge or sharpened mystery |

A chapter can defer payoff, but it must supply recognizable progress. Repetition is not progress unless pressure, meaning, or cost changes.

## 5. Scene and sequel

Dwight V. Swain organizes fiction through scenes and sequels [S4]. Jack Bickham develops scene structure as a way to create logical narrative flow [S5]. Randy Ingermanson labels the action unit goal–conflict–setback and the processing unit reaction–dilemma–decision [S6].

Use neutral names to avoid formula worship:

- **Action unit:** goal/question → resistance → outcome.
- **Processing unit:** felt consequence → options/meaning → decision.

A unit can be one line, a paragraph, or several scenes. A chapter may interleave them. Missing processing is a problem only when the next choice becomes emotionally or causally illegible.

### Compression test

For each processing passage:

1. What new feeling, interpretation, option, or decision appears?
2. Could one sentence carry the same change?
3. Does the duration match the wound or revelation being processed?

For each action passage:

1. Does the goal remain live?
2. Does resistance force choice rather than delay only?
3. Does the outcome alter the state?

## 6. Try–fail cycles

Sanderson presents try–fail as intelligent attempts that expose limits, antagonist strength, or missing knowledge, while warning against rigid frameworks [S8]. The useful pattern is not “fail three times”; it is **attempt → distinct consequence → adaptation**.

| Pass | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Escalating | cost, time, danger, or personal stake increases |
| Differentiated | failure reason is new |
| Adaptive | next method uses what was learned |
| Character-bearing | flaw, value, or relationship affects method |
| Paid off | success uses earned knowledge/coordination |

**Failure modes:**

- cosmetic retries that change only wording;
- characters ignore obvious prior knowledge;
- failure exists only to fill pages;
- final success introduces an unseeded capability;
- the emotional lesson is stated again after action has proved it.

## 7. SF/F quest causality

The SFWA Turkey City Lexicon names “And plot” for event chains that add up to nothing, “Bogus Alternatives” for stopping action to list unused choices, and “Plot Coupons” for quest objects accumulated without organic dramatic pressure [S19]. Use these labels carefully and explain the actual reader effect.

In a quest or survival story, every travel or repair beat should do at least one of:


- change survival tactics;
- alter trust or family bonds;
- expose a world rule through consequence;
- progress the central search or quest goal;
- raise the cost of the next choice.

## 8. Structural model selection

| Symptom | Best first lens |
|---|---|
| chapter begins late or ends twice | causal wholeness / opening-ending |
| middle spins | promise–progress–payoff |
| choices feel unmotivated | action/processing units |
| repeated obstacles | try–fail differentiation |
| huge climax but weak aftermath | tension/release |
| quest feels episodic | SF/F causality |
| act turn lacks force | three-act or Freytag, then causality |

No framework supplies a verdict by itself. Cite prose, explain effect, and propose the smallest complete fix.
