# Sources

Accessed 14 November 2026. Sources are ranked toward professional standards, university-press craft, peer-reviewed
research, and established professional organizations.

## Professional editing and story-bible practice

**S1. Editors’ Association of Canada.** *Professional Editorial Standards 2024.* 2024.
https://editors.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/EditorsCanada_ProfessionalEditorialStandards_2024.pdf

Relevant standards: stay within scope and preserve intended meaning (A2.5); recognize/query questionable accuracy,
including fictional anachronisms (B2.2); ensure consistent point of view/voice/register (C3.1). Official national
professional standard.

**S2. Amy J. Schneider.** *The Chicago Guide to Copyediting Fiction.* University of Chicago Press, 2023.
https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/C/bo183690033.html
DOI: https://doi.org/10.7208/chicago/9780226823027.001.0001

Chapters 4–6 cover character, place, and timeline style-sheet fields. Publisher descriptions specify continuity
tracking for characters, places, events, distances, routes, fixed/relative time, travel, parallel timelines, POV
changes, and flashbacks; complex contradictions go back to the author.

**S3. Chartered Institute of Editing and Proofreading (CIEP).** “Creating style sheets for fiction.” 27 July 2023.
https://www.ciep.uk/resource/fiction-style-sheets.html

Institutional practitioner guidance: plot notes by chapter; character/place/world facts; and a timeline,
particularly for nonlinear stories.

**S4. Chartered Institute of Editing and Proofreading (CIEP).** “What is copyediting?”
https://www.ciep.uk/resource/what-is-copyediting.html

Institutional scope guidance: in fiction, check stable character details, coherent timeline and fictional world,
and consistent narrative voice; maintain a style sheet and query what cannot be confirmed.

**S5. Deanna Hoak.** “The Importance of Style Sheets.” Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers Association,
4 February 2010. https://www.sfwa.org/2010/02/04/the-importance-of-style-sheets/

Experienced SF/F copyeditor's practice: build and consult the sheet during editing; record characters, places,
invented terms, and first occurrences; series and speculative fiction especially need continuity records.

**S6. Terry McGarry.** “A Writer’s Guide to Understanding the Copyeditor.” *SFWA Bulletin*, Spring 1995;
republished by SFWA 23 June 2009.
https://sfwa.org/2009/06/23/a-writers-guide-to-understanding-the-copyeditor/

Experienced SF copyeditor's practice: look facts up; maintain occurrence lists; visualize action; query impossible
or internally inconsistent events; preserve authorial style and intent.

**S7. Rochelle Melander.** “The Story Bible: What It Is … and Why You Need One.” Excerpt from *Write-A-Thon*,
Jane Friedman, 17 October 2011.
https://janefriedman.com/the-story-bible/

Practitioner overview of a living story bible as a compact source for characters, settings, plot, research, and
continuity. Use as secondary craft support, not as an authority over manuscript evidence.

## Narrative time and reader/character knowledge

**S8. Encyclopaedia Britannica editors.** “Dramatic irony.”
https://www.britannica.com/art/dramatic-irony

Defines dramatic irony as a structural gap in which audience/reader understanding exceeds character
understanding and changes the meaning of character words/actions.

**S9. Cynthia Cabañas, Atsushi Senju, and Tim J. Smith.** “The audience who knew too much: investigating the role
of spontaneous theory of mind on the processing of dramatic irony scenes in film.” *Frontiers in Psychology* 14
(2023): 1183660. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1183660
Open text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10353302/

Proof-of-concept experiment: participants given critical information unavailable to characters used more epistemic
mental-state language during the exploitation scene. Supports explicit installation/exploitation and belief-state
tracking; it does not prescribe prose technique.

**S10. Rolf A. Zwaan, Gabriel A. Radvansky, Amy E. Hilliard, and Jacqueline M. Curiel.** “Constructing
Multidimensional Situation Models During Reading.” *Scientific Studies of Reading* 2.3 (1998): 199–220.
https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532799xssr0203_2

Experiments tested time, space, causation, motivation/goals, and protagonist continuity. Reading time increased as
situational continuity breaks accumulated; spatial effects depended on map knowledge. Supports a multidimensional
ledger, not a universal severity formula.

**S11. Susan A. J. Birch and Paul Bloom.** “The Curse of Knowledge in Reasoning About False Beliefs.”
*Psychological Science* 18.5 (2007): 382–386.
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01909.x
PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576275/

Adults' own outcome knowledge can compromise judgments of another person's false belief. Supports bounded
reader/character checks at the earlier chapter rather than retrospective intuition.

**S12. Gérard Genette.** *Narrative Discourse: An Essay in Method.* Translated by Jane E. Lewin. Cornell
University Press, 1979.
Publisher record: https://books.google.com/books/about/Narrative_Discourse.html?id=yEPuQg7SOxIC

Foundational narratology distinguishes story chronology from narrative order and analyzes order, duration, and
frequency. Use those distinctions to keep flashback, summary, scene, and repeated narration separate from the
in-world clock.
