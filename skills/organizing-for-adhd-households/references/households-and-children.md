# Couples, teens and children (detail)

## Couples: one parent with ADHD, one without

Common loop: one person compensates, resentment grows, nagging begins, the other withdraws.
Sources call it a parent-child trap. [popular claim from clinician/coach sites; plausible, not tested]

Steps:
1. Agree on a weekly 10-minute check-in. Use feelings ("I feel stretched") not accusations.
2. Divide by zone and preference, not 50/50 by task. Let each person rank chores by dislike.
3. Move reminders from voice to visible: shared board, shared list, shared calendar.
4. Do short chore sprints side by side (about 15 minutes, timer).
5. Cut the workload first: automation, delivery, fewer possessions.
6. The non-ADHD partner drops the supervisor role; the ADHD partner repeats back agreements
   and makes small daily gestures of partnership.
7. Persistent resentment: suggest an ADHD-informed couples therapist or coach. [professional practice]

## Children and teens: who touches what

### Teenager
- **Developmentally normal:** messy rooms, privacy, and identity expressed through space. Friction is
  often about control and trust, not the mess. [popular claim; psychology article]
- **Parents own:** shared spaces, hygiene basics (food rot, laundry in the hamper at agreed times),
  safety (pests, fire, medication), and agreed house rules.
- **Teen owns:** layout, decor, how they store clothes and papers inside their room, and their own
  schedule systems.
- **Parent should not:** clean it for them, rummage without cause, lecture, or treat mess as
  disrespect. Cleaning up for a teen teaches that mess eventually gets rescued.
- **Fade support** deliberately: parents stay involved but hand over responsibility step by step
  (Boston Children's / CHADD). [clinical]
- **Let them choose the fix** after a mistake; offer brainstorming, not solutions. [clinical]
- **Offer systems, not rules:** a weekly 15-minute planning session, visible timers, a set place for
  everything, phone out of the bedroom at night. [clinical/professional practice]
- Persistent change in a teen's tidiness plus low mood or withdrawal is worth a gentle check-in and,
  if it continues, a conversation with a doctor or counselor. Do not interpret it yourself.

Script to a teen: "This is your room. The deal is: food and dishes out, laundry in the hamper on
laundry day, nothing blocking the door. Everything else is yours. How do you want to remind yourself?"

### Younger children, including significant support needs
- **Ask the family's OT or therapist first.** Sensory needs vary widely; assessment tells what
  helps. Do not buy sensory equipment before that. [clinical]
- **Predictable structure**: each area has a clear purpose, labeled with pictures or words, and items
  live in the same place. Visual schedules show what comes next and give warning of changes. This
  is the "structured supports" approach. [professional practice, Aspect]
- **Visual timers** for transitions; try several visual formats and keep the one the child reads.
- **Lower visual clutter** where the child sleeps and regulates; closed storage can reduce
  visual noise; rotating a small set of toys is common advice (effort cost to parents). [popular claim]
- **Calm corner/regulation space** is widely used; build it with the child, not for them. Observe how
  each activity affects them; what calms one can over-excite another (Understood).
- **"Heavy work"** (push, pull, carry, climb) can regulate some children; keep it short; an OT
  sets the plan (Understood). [clinical]
- **Child-height access:** hooks and open bins at their reach, with picture labels, so they can
  put away independently. [professional practice]
- **Safety first for wandering/elopement risk:** door/window alarms and stop-sign cues are
  common; drowning is the main danger. Locks on exits can conflict with fire escape; check with the
  local fire department before adding any lock to an exit door (Spark for Autism).
  [clinical, safety]
- **Medical equipment, medications and supplies:** keep a labeled, reachable, locked-as-needed
  station away from sensory-sensitive areas; the care team decides what must be kept. Ask the
  family's clinicians; I found no authoritative organizing guide for this. [unverified]
- **Caregiver load:** families with high care needs have little capacity left; default to
  tiny sessions, hands-on help, and zero new maintenance steps.
- Watch for conflict: a visually calm home for one child may be a "no-touch" home that frustrates
  another. Negotiate zones.
