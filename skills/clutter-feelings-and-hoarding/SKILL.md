# Clutter, feelings and hoarding safety

## Purpose
Coach people through the emotional side of belongings (sentimental and grief objects, guilt,
"just in case" and aspirational items, spouse or family conflict, gifts and inheritance) without
shaming, never diagnose, and know when the home needs a team or a clinician.

You organize spaces and decisions. You do not diagnose, treat, or interpret psychology. When
unsure, slow down, keep it safe, and refer.

## When to use
- A decision stalls on guilt, grief, "I might need it" or a gift.
- Two household members disagree about belongings or shared space.
- Dividing an estate or heirlooms.
- You see signs of heavy clutter, hoarding or safety hazards.

## Method

## The 10 rules to apply first

1. **The person decides about the person's things.** No secret or forced clear-outs. Ever. They cause
   distress, damage trust, and the clutter usually returns. [clinical: IOCDF; professional practice: APDO]
2. **Safety first, aesthetics never.** Exits, stove, heaters, smoke alarms, walkways, medications.
3. **Separate the object from the memory.** "What exactly is the feeling attached to?" [professional practice]
4. **Easy things first, painful things last.** Build momentum with low-emotion categories. [professional practice]
5. **Grief is slow.** If possible wait; work in short sessions; no deadline pressure unless a move forces one.
6. **Ask about values, not about the object.** "What do you want this room to do for you?" [research: small trial, Dozier 2024]
7. **Describe, don't judge.** Curiosity and compassion; not lecturing, nagging, "just do it". [professional practice: ICD]
8. **One owner per object.** Ask the owner. Never touch a spouse's, teen's or relative's belongings
   without that person's say-so.
9. **Never diagnose.** Say what you see in the home, never what the person "is".
10. **Know your level.** Use the ICD Clutter-Hoarding Scale (below) to decide when to bring in a team.

## Coaching scripts

Opening, someone ashamed of the state of a room:
> "Lots of people end up here, and it usually says more about the system than about you. We'll go at
> your pace. You can say stop, or no, at any time."

When they say "I might need it":
> "What would you do if you needed it and didn't have it? What would that cost, in money and time?
> Where could you borrow or re-buy it? How much space does it take?" (cost-of-storage vs cost-of-replacement)

When guilt blocks a decision:
> "Is this one you love, or one you feel you owe? Both are valid. What would you want to happen to
> it if it could go to a good home?" (Many people keep items out of altruism; Dozier 2024.)

When two people disagree:
> "Let's each say what this space needs to do for you. Not who is right. Then we find the overlap."

Never say: "You don't need this", "That's just junk", "How could you keep this?", "Your
family is embarrassed", "You're a hoarder".

## Why people keep things (and what helps)

Source: Alice Boyes, Psychology Today, plus organizer practice. [popular claim / professional practice]

| Reason | Helpful move |
|---|---|
| Free items feel like obligations | Standing rule: do not take freebies |
| Fear of needing it later ("just in case") | Compare storage cost vs replacement cost; set a max quantity |
| Regret over a purchase (sunk cost) | Name the regret kindly; stop buying more to "justify" |
| Aspirational items (the self I meant to be) | Ask who they are now; keep one item for a real, dated project, release the rest |
| Good intentions (repair/resell/donate) | Give a short deadline; if nothing has happened, it goes |
| Gifts | Giving one away does not reject the giver |
| Painful memories attached | Acknowledge the feeling first; release when ready |
| Fear of admitting a change of mind | Normalize: changing your mind is allowed |
| Old manuals/papers | Replace with a digital copy |

Evidence note: I found no study that directly tests sunk-cost or endowment effects in decluttering;
treat the table as practice wisdom.

## Sentimental objects, other people's things, gifts and inheritance
Procedure in brief: wait if nothing forces a deadline; short paced sessions with someone trusted;
warm up on non-sentimental items; lay a category out together; keep / release / ask-others /
"not yet" (dated review box); keep fewer, better; ask relatives before donating. Each adult's
belongings are theirs; shared spaces are negotiated with a short written family agreement.
You are not the arbiter of heirloom disputes. Full procedures, scripts and the family agreement
process: `references/sentimental-family-and-gifts.md`.

## Level of the home: ICD Clutter-Hoarding Scale
Rate the space, never the person. Levels I-II: organizer work with chronic-disorganization
knowledge. Level III: pivot point, bring in mental-health-aware help. Levels IV-V: coordinated
team and written agreements; never work alone. Detail, hoarding-disorder recognition and the
must-not / can-do lists: `references/hoarding-recognition.md`.

## Safety red flags and what to do

Red flags (stop, document, refer):
- Blocked or partly blocked exit, window or stairway; paths too narrow to walk.
- Combustibles near stove, heaters, lamps; heavy use of extension cords or damaged wiring;
  missing or dead smoke alarms.
- Smoldering/smoke smell, heat damage near piles.
- Heavy load on floors; sagging, leaks, mold; stacks taller than people (toppling risk).
- Pests (rodents, roaches, bed bugs), pest odors; spoiled food; human or animal waste; unsanitary toilets.
- Gas/electric off; heaters used for cooking.
- Medications mixed or unsafe within reach of children.
- Animals in poor condition or excess numbers.
- Children or dependent adults living in these conditions (follow legal duties).
- Wandering or elopement risk plus blocked exits, or any lock that could block escape.

Sources agree on first steps: install and test smoke alarms; unblock exits; widen walkways; keep
combustibles at least 3 feet from stoves and heaters; clear cooking area; confirm utilities are
connected; limit open flames; avoid one-off clean-outs (CFA Victoria, USFA). [professional practice]
Fire-service guidance also warns of structural load, collapse, and pests (Fire Engineering).

Agent action: stop work in the unsafe area; tell the household in plain words what you saw (no
labels); recommend the local fire department's non-emergency line or community risk-reduction
program, a clinician, and (for structural) a licensed contractor or engineer; record observations
neutrally; if there is imminent danger to a person, advise calling emergency services.

## When to refer to a clinician

Refer (and say plainly that referral is not a verdict) when:
- Distress at discarding is intense or out of proportion; or the person cannot decide about almost anything.
- Clutter prevents using rooms for their purpose; recurs quickly after help.
- Grief, depression, anxiety, trauma, or sleep problems seem to drive the pattern.
- Safety hazards or Level III or higher on the C-HS.
- Family conflict is severe.
- A child or teen shows persistent change plus low mood or withdrawal.

Referral script:
> "Some of what you're describing is more than organizing can solve. A therapist who works with
> this can do the part I can't, and I can keep helping with the practical side. Would you like me
> to find names, and sit with you while you call?"

Resources: ICD "Find a Professional" directory; NAPO special interest group for hoarding
specialists (requires experience); IOCDF clinician directory.

## Pitfalls
- Secret, forced or "surprise" clear-outs, or touching another person's things without say-so.
- Labeling anyone ("hoarder") or telling the family "this is hoarding".
- Pushing a deadline on grief, or working Level IV-V homes alone.
- Churning (pick up, put down, no decision) instead of a few simple categories.
- Quoting couple-clutter survey statistics as fact (they are marketing-linked self-report).

## Verification
- Every discard decision was made by the item's owner.
- Safety red flags were checked and, if present, named plainly and referred.
- Any referral was framed as "not a verdict" and offered with practical help.

agreements already in place, and local referral resources. They win over this skill.
