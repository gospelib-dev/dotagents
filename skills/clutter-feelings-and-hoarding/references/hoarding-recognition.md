# Clutter-Hoarding Scale and hoarding recognition

## ICD Clutter-Hoarding Scale (C-HS)

An observational scale for the condition of a home's interior. It rates the space, not the person; it
is not for diagnosis. Free for educational/non-profit use with credit; commercial use needs ICD
permission. I opened the 2011 quick guide (hosted by a city) and the 2025 quick guide (hosted by ICD).
Wording differs slightly; use the current ICD version. [professional practice]

Five categories: structure and zoning (exits, plumbing/electric/HVAC, structural integrity);
animals and pests; household functions; health and safety (sanitation, medications); personal
protective equipment.

| Level | Color / label | ICD guidance (paraphrased) |
|---|---|---|
| I | Green, low | Standard household; little or no clutter; chronic-disorganization knowledge still helps |
| II | Blue, guarded | Evidence of clutter; specific CD knowledge always required |
| III | Yellow, elevated | Pivot point between cluttered and possible hoarding environment; advanced training and a network, especially mental health professionals |
| IV | Orange, high | Coordinated team: organizer plus health, social work, legal/financial, pest/animal control, biohazard cleaners, contractors; advanced hoarding knowledge |
| V | Red, severe | Intervention; formal written agreements among the parties first; legal/housing proceedings common; 2011 guide: organizers should not work alone |

How to use it: you may use it to decide what help the home needs. Never use it to label a person.

## Recognizing hoarding disorder (recognition only)

Description from the APA and IOCDF: persistent difficulty parting with possessions because of
distress about discarding, leading to clutter that stops rooms being used as intended, with
distress or impairment. Often involves strong urges to save and excess acquiring. [clinical]
Roughly 2-3% of people. Best-supported treatment is CBT delivered by a clinician. Severe clutter and
chronic disorganization are not the same thing; most chronic disorganization is not hoarding
disorder (IOCDF, ICD). Hoarding often co-occurs with depression, anxiety, ADHD. [clinical]

Say, if concerned: "I'm not able to judge what's behind this, but a specialist could help.
Would you like a list of clinicians who work with difficulty letting go of things?"
Never say: "You have hoarding disorder."

### What the organizer must not do
- Diagnose, label, or tell the family "this is hoarding".
- Clear out without clear, informed consent, or "surprise" clean-outs, or remove items "for their own good".
- Argue, shame, lecture, pressure someone to discard before they are ready, or push for a spotless result.
- Work alone at ICD Levels IV-V, or ignore structural or health hazards.
- Take on treatment tasks (cognitive/emotional work); that belongs to a clinician. [professional practice; APA/IOCDF describe CBT by clinicians as the treatment]
- Keep unsafe conditions a secret from those who must know for safety, if consent is given; follow
  local law on abuse, neglect, or dependent children or adults at risk.
- Use churning (pick up, put down, no decision); give the person a few simple categories.
- Keep supporting the pile (storage payments, collecting items) without agreeing a plan. (IOCDF)

### What you can do
- Build trust; be trauma-informed and non-judgmental (APDO).
- Work at the person's pace; the goal is function and safety, not a clear house.
- Use harm reduction: safer home, even if still cluttered. Frame requests as safety ("can we clear
  this path to the door?"), not appearance. Involve them in the safety plan. (IOCDF)
- Use motivational interviewing questions: "What do you want your home to be able to do?"
- Contact the person's therapist if they consent; coordinate. (IOCDF)
- Expect setbacks; focus on trend.
- Prepare a written agreement and team before Level IV-V work.
