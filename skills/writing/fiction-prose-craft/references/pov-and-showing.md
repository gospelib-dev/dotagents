# POV, psychic distance, show vs tell

## Psychic distance (John Gardner, The Art of Fiction, 1983)
The five levels, quoted via Emma Darwin's essay "Psychic Distance: what it is and how to use it":
1. It was winter of the year 1853. A large man stepped out of a doorway.
2. Henry J. Warburton had never much cared for snowstorms.
3. Henry hated snowstorms.
4. God how he hated these damn snowstorms.
5. Snow. Under your collar, down inside your shoes, freezing and plugging up your miserable soul…

Darwin's points that matter here:
- A jump straight from PD1 to PD5 can lose the reader, who cannot yet confirm whose experience it is.
- PD4 corresponds to free indirect style and PD5 to stream of consciousness.
- Distance is a dial you turn during a scene, not a fixed setting.

Typical mapping for close third person:
- PD2 for transits and time skips ("Two days of rain went by").
- PD3–4 for most scenes.
- PD5 at peaks only, and only when the images it compresses were planted earlier.

## Filtering (Gardner, quoted in Burroway, Writing Fiction)
"Vividness urges that almost every occurrence of such phrases as 'she noticed' and 'she saw' be suppressed in
favor of direct presentation of the thing seen." Gardner's example: "Turning, she noticed two snakes fighting
in among the rocks" becomes "She turned. In among the rocks, two snakes were fighting."

Exceptions (Author magazine, "Fending Off Filters", 2010):
- to re-establish whose eyes we are behind;
- when the noticing is itself the point (she *saw* him hide the pill).


## Thought verbs (Chuck Palahniuk, "Nuts and Bolts: 'Thought' Verbs", LitReactor)
- Banned for practice: thinks, knows, understands, realizes, believes, wants, remembers, imagines, desires,
  loves, hates.
- Un-pack each one into physical detail "so that the reader wants it".
- A thought verb at the start of a paragraph works as a "thesis statement" that steals the thunder of
  what follows. Cut it, or move it after the evidence.
- Characters alone drift into thinking. Put them with another character fast.

## R.U.E. (Renni Browne & Dave King, Self-Editing for Fiction Writers, 2nd ed. 2004, ch. 1 and ch. 5)
- "Resist the Urge to Explain." When you find an explanation of an emotion, cut it. If the emotion is still
  shown, the explanation was not needed. If it is not shown, rewrite the passage so that it is.
- Explaining an emotion after the dialogue has already shown it is like the playwright running on stage
  to explain the scene to the audience.
- Showing too far (no summary at all) reads breathless. An occasional paragraph of narrative summary
  gives depth (Dave King interview, Writer Unboxed, 2007).

## Motivation-reaction units (Swain, Techniques of the Selling Writer, ch. 3)
- Order: motivating stimulus, then character reaction, and within the reaction: (1) feeling, (2) action,
  (3) speech. "Motivation always precedes reaction."
- Swain's example: "Far across on the hillside, a shot rang out. John stiffened" reads better than "John
  stiffened when a shot rang out…".
- Components may be omitted. Omitting feeling is the riskiest, because the action can then be misread.
- Each stimulus must have significance to the character, pertinence to the story and motivity for the
  reader. A spotlighted stimulus is a promise ("false plant" if unused).
