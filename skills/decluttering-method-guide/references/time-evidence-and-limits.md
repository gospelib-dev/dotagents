# Time, clinical boundaries and research evidence

### Realistic time per room (planning ranges, not guarantees)
Sources are organizer blogs; treat as order of magnitude. A Bay Area organizer reports 5-hour
sessions and about 2-3 days of solid work for an average room excluding closets; other
public pages cite 3-8 hours for a room, with kitchens, basements, and garages longer [professional
practice, unverified aggregate]. For a family doing it themselves, double the pro time.
Planning table (solo, self-directed, moderate clutter):
- Bathroom, entry, single closet, one drawer cluster: 1-2 hours.
- Bedroom or kids' room: 3-6 hours (2 sessions).
- Kitchen: counters and cabinets 4-8 hours; pantry plus cabinets can reach 12 hours in
  heavy cases. Two to four sessions.
- Living room/office with paper: 4-8 hours; paper alone 3-6 hours.
- Garage/basement/attic: 8-20+ hours across sessions plus a dump/donation run.
Factors that lengthen: years of accumulation, chronic disorganization or hoarding,
sentimental items, disagreement between people, homework not done between sessions.
Rule: schedule the first two sessions back-to-back, then weekly, so momentum forms.

## 6. When this is not just clutter

- The ICD Clutter-Hoarding Scale has five levels (I minimal to V extreme) and is a
  home-observation tool for safety, sanitation, and usability; it is not a diagnosis.
  Level III is the midpoint; Level IV needs a coordinated team (contractors, fire/safety
  agencies) [professional practice]. A companion Clutter Quality of Life Scale asks the
  person how clutter affects them.
- Hoarding disorder (DSM-5): persistent difficulty parting with items regardless of value,
  distress at discarding, clutter that blocks living areas. Estimated prevalence 2-5% of
  adults, onset usually in youth, commonly co-occurring with depression, anxiety, ADHD
  and autism [research, via summary]. Treatment evidence points to CBT with home sessions,
  homework and motivational work; harm reduction helps when insight is low [research].
- Organizers help with practical skills but are not clinicians. IOCDF says organizers can
  teach sorting, categorizing, routines, OHIO, and a "home inside the home" for each item;
  ideally they coordinate with the client's therapist [professional practice].
- Forced clean-outs: a county guide and several secondary reports describe distress,
  self-harm risk, and refusal of future help. I could not open the primary documents
  [popular claim until verified].
Agent rule: if safety flags or heavy distress appear, switch to harm-reduction goals
(clear exits, bed, stove, sink), slow the pace, never remove items without the owner's
explicit consent, and recommend a CD-trained organizer or clinician.
Chronic disorganization is a working concept in the field, not a diagnosis, and ICD says
it persists despite repeated self-help attempts and often co-occurs with ADHD, anxiety,
depression, and other conditions. ADHD note: sources I found were blogs; recommended
tactics (visible open storage, body doubling, external timers, micro-tasks) are common
in practice but untested here [popular claim].

## 8. Research evidence, graded

| Claim | Status | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Clutter linked to stress | [research], weak | Saxbe & Repetti 2010: 30 dual-income couples, home-tour language, daily cortisol; stressful-home description in women linked to flatter cortisol patterns and low mood, not in men; correlational, small |
| Clutter linked to lower life satisfaction | [research], weak | Roster et al. 2016: ~1,400-1,500 clutter-troubled adults recruited via a disorganization nonprofit; self-selected, cross-sectional |
| Clutter and procrastination | [research], weak | Ferrari et al. 2018: 346 students; link exists, direction unknown |
| Clutter competes for attention | [research] | McMains & Kastner 2011 (fMRI): multiple visual stimuli suppress each other's neural responses; attention offsets this; lab perception study, not a household trial |
| Orderliness shifts behavior | [research] | Vohs et al. 2013: tidy rooms -> healthier snacks and more giving; messy rooms -> more creative ideas (abstract-level only, I did not open full text) |
| Tidying causes well-being | no direct study found | a media-research review concluded none tests tidying or KonMari over time |
| Decision fatigue | contested | ego-depletion 23-lab replication (N about 2,100) found no effect; critics say task was weak; parole-judge data is real but interpreted by a depletion theory in doubt. Practical advice (short sessions, breaks, hard items early or after warmup) is [professional practice], not proven |
| Choice overload | contested | meta-analysis of 50 experiments (5,036 people): mean effect near zero, big variance |
| Endowment effect | [research], robust but debated | Kahneman, Knetsch, Thaler 1990: owners demanded far more than non-owners would pay; trading volume lower than predicted; critics argue design artifacts, weaker for substitutable goods |
| Sunk cost | [research] | Arkes & Blumer 1985: continuing after investment; debiasing = evaluate only future costs/benefits |
| Habit formation | [research] | Lally et al. 2010: 96 adults, 84 days; median 66 days (range 18-254) in 39 well-fitting cases; one missed day no big effect; exercise slower; not "21 days" |
| If-then plans help | [research] | implementation-intention studies show gains across voting, flu shots, dieting; can backfire for strong perfectionists |

- Not verified: ICD's own ethics/standards text; Morgenstern and Walsh primary books; the
  Home Edit method; Saxbe, Roster, Vohs full texts; primary hoarding clean-out data.
