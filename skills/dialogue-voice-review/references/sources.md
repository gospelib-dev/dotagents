# Sources

Sources are keyed `[S#]` in the skill. Operational thresholds in the skill are house review triggers, not claims made by these works.


1. **[S1] Ursula K. Le Guin.** *Steering the Craft: A Twenty-First-Century Guide to Sailing the Sea of Story*. Revised edition, Mariner/Harper Perennial, 2015. Author page: https://www.ursulakleguin.com/steering-the-craft — sound, sentence construction, repetition, point of view, and voice as learnable craft.
2. **[S2] Robert McKee.** *Dialogue: The Art of Verbal Action for the Page, Stage, and Screen*. Twelve, 2016. Official author page: https://mckeestory.com/books/dialogue — dialogue answers a need, serves a purpose, and performs action; text operates over subtext.
3. **[S3] Shawn Bird and Vickey M. Giles.** “Honoring the Student Voices in Young Adult Literature.” *Voices from the Middle* 18.3 (2011), 53–54. NCTE. https://doi.org/10.58680/vm201113568 — teen readers detect adult imitation; authenticity matters.
4. **[S4] Megan Frazer Blakemore.** “Writing Dialogue,” guest lesson on Kate Messner’s *Teachers Write*, 2014. https://katemessner.com/teachers-write-8414-mini-lesson-monday/ — true rather than transcript-real dialogue; age/background specificity; read-aloud, speech-length, slang, contraction, tag, and physical-task revision checks.
5. **[S5] Adele K. Wallis, Marleen F. Westerveld, Allison M. Waters, and Pamela C. Snow.** “Investigating Adolescent Discourse in Critical Thinking: Monologic Responses to Stories Containing a Moral Dilemma.” *Language, Speech, and Hearing Services in Schools* 52 (2021). https://doi.org/10.1044/2020_LSHSS-20-00134 — younger/older adolescent reasoning differences; cautions against equating age with simple syntax.
6. **[S6] Salomi S. Asaridou, O. Ece Demir-Lira, Julia Uddén, Susan Goldin-Meadow, and Steven L. Small.** “Pragmatic Language Processing in the Adolescent Brain.” 2019 manuscript. https://cpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/voices.uchicago.edu/dist/c/1286/files/2020/02/Asaridou-Demir-Lira-Udden-GM-and-Small-2019.pdf — adolescent context-sensitive indirect speech and heightened processing of affective replies.
7. **[S7] V. Lord Larson and N. L. McKinley.** “Characteristics of Adolescents’ Conversations: A Longitudinal Study.” *Clinical Linguistics & Phonetics* 12.3 (1998), 183–203. https://doi.org/10.3109/02699209808985221 — peer/adult audience effects, question types, figurative language, topic shifts, entertainment and persuasion functions; small longitudinal sample.
8. **[S8] American Speech-Language-Hearing Association.** “Social Communication Benchmarks.” https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/social-communication-disorder/social-communication-benchmarks/ — descriptive pragmatic-development ranges and explicit cultural, linguistic, and neurodiversity caution.
9. **[S9] Anna-Brita Stenström.** “Avoid Silence! Keep Talking!: Pragmatic Markers as Phatic Devices in Teenage Conversation.” *Functions of Language* 21.1 (2014), 30–49. https://doi.org/10.1075/fol.21.1.03ste — marker choice distinguishes bonding from informative talk better than simple frequency in the studied extracts.
10. **[S10] Krishnapriya Vishnubhotla, Adam Hammond, and Graeme Hirst.** “Are Fictional Voices Distinguishable? Classifying Character Voices in Modern Drama.” *Proceedings of the 3rd Joint SIGHUM Workshop* (ACL, 2019), 29–34. https://aclanthology.org/W19-2504/ — lexical and syntactic features can probabilistically distinguish fictional speakers; findings are preliminary/domain-limited.
11. **[S11] Kelvin K. H. Lee.** “‘Watashi-tachi wa ningen da!’: A Corpus-Assisted Analysis of a Non-Human Character in the Anime Series ‘From the New World’.” *New Voices in Japanese Studies* 10 (2018), 52–75. https://doi.org/10.21159/nvjs.10.03 — linguistic repertoire and meaningful style shifts construct non-human identity and persona.
12. **[S12] Brandon Sanderson, Mary Robinette Kowal, Dan Wells, and Howard Tayler.** “Making Non-Human Characters Relatable.” *Writing Excuses* 8.33 (2013). https://writingexcuses.com/writing-excuses-8-33-making-non-human-characters-relatable/ — familiar need as reader bridge; contrast it with a purposeful non-human feature or communication mode.
13. **[S13] Christopher Hoffmann.** “What Your Choice of Dialogue Tags Says About You.” Jane Friedman, 2020. https://janefriedman.com/dialogue-tags/ — unobtrusive attribution, redundancy, non-speech verbs, and genre-sensitive marked tags.
14. **[S14] Alex Limberg.** “Balancing Dialogue and Description in Your Story.” Jane Friedman, 2015. https://janefriedman.com/balancing-dialogue-and-description/ — description and beats should orient, characterize, create mood, block action, or advance the scene.
15. **[S15] Purdue Online Writing Lab.** “Quotation Marks with Fiction.” Purdue University. https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/quotation_marks/quotation_marks_with_fiction.html — paragraphing speaker changes and standard dialogue-tag punctuation.
