# Tension, release, hooks, and audience calibration

## Tension model

Lehne and Koelsch model tension as predictive processing triggered by conflict, instability, dissonance, or uncertainty about future events of emotional significance [S9]. For practical review, track six levers:

1. **Stake:** what valued outcome can be gained or lost?
2. **Imminence:** how soon must action occur?
3. **Instability:** what cannot remain as it is?
4. **Agency:** can the character act, and at what cost?
5. **Knowledge:** what do character and reader know or misread?
6. **Investment:** why does this outcome matter emotionally?

Rate tension 0–10 only to compare beats within the same chapter. A “7” is not objectively measurable.

### Tension is not uncertainty alone

Brewer and Lichtenstein’s structural-affect theory distinguishes suspense, curiosity, and surprise as effects of discourse arrangement [S10]. Hoeken and van Vliet experimentally found suspense even when readers knew the outcome, and found surprise improved appreciation and recall in their story conditions [S11]. Therefore:

- do not “fix” foreshadowing merely because it reveals likely outcome;
- ask whether anticipation, empathy, and process remain charged;
- use curiosity (“how/why?”) and suspense (“what will happen?”) deliberately;
- let a surprise cause reassessment rather than appear uncaused.

## Release model

Release lets the reader register consequence. It may be:

| Release | What changes |
|---|---|
| physiological | breath, pain, fatigue, sensory reorientation |
| cognitive | interpretation, plan, or recognition |
| relational | touch, joke, refusal, confession, silence |
| tactical | inventory, regrouping, changed route |
| imagistic | one concrete image carrying aftermath |

A release sags when it restates the peak without changing meaning. A release is rushed when a major loss is followed by a new action before body, mind, or relationship can register it.

Donald Maass’s practitioner concept of “micro-tension” usefully asks whether even quiet pages contain emotional friction [S15]. Use it as a question, not as a demand for constant overt conflict.

## Pace versus prose speed

- Short sentences can accelerate local reading texture; they cannot replace goal, consequence, or stakes.
- Long sentences can slow attention or produce breathless accumulation depending on syntax and context.
- Summary can accelerate elapsed time while reflection deepens emotional time.
- Scene can feel fast at 1,500 words if the state changes often; 300 words can drag if nothing changes.

Measure beat duration, change frequency, and mode function before judging sentence texture.

## Openings

Nancy Kress emphasizes gaining attention early, keeping the middle tight, and delivering on the story’s implicit promise [S13]. Les Edgerton treats inciting incident, story-worthy problem, opening length, transitions, and backstory control as opening craft [S14].

**Opening test:** within the first 10% or 500 words, can the review state:

- what is different now;
- what the viewpoint character wants or fears;
- what question or pressure organizes the chapter;
- why this chapter starts here rather than one beat later?

A quiet opening can pass through desire, unease, promise, or relationship change. Do not require danger.

## Chapter endings and hooks

Hook types:

- danger or interrupted action;
- decision/commitment;
- revelation or reinterpretation;
- consequence/cost;
- emotional rupture or bond;
- image with forward implication;
- explicit unanswered question.

Schibler’s two experiments found cliffhanger endings increased desire for a future installment relative to resolved victory endings, but did not significantly increase reported suspense or enjoyment [S12]. Therefore cliffhangers are one retention tool, not an all-purpose quality rule.

**Ending test:**

1. What local promise is paid or deliberately carried?
2. What has changed since the opening?
3. What exact question, consequence, or intention pulls forward?
4. Does material after the hook deepen it, or create a second ending?
5. Do adjacent chapters repeat the same hook intensity or construction?

## Audience calibration (YA and SF/F as the worked case)

YALSA’s official teen-selection framing covers ages 12–18 [S16]. Deborah Halverson’s YA guide emphasizes teen voice, dialogue, character, and plot [S18]; Mary Kole’s kidlit guide emphasizes matching tone, length, content, theme, and plot structure to YA/MG readership [S17]. These are audience lenses, not simplification orders.

- Keep cause and spatial action legible during technology or nature hazards.
- Give emotional consequence enough room to land for the target reader without re-explaining the lesson.
- Teach world rules through pressure, sensory evidence, and choice; keep seeds short.
- Alternate danger, discovery, relationship movement, and processing according to chapter function.
- Keep disability or adaptation arcs effortful; do not accelerate by erasing failed attempts or dignity.
- Distinguish manuscript-level market length from chapter-level pace.

Mary Kole’s current practitioner guidance gives roughly 100,000 words as a YA SF/F ceiling and warns that long work may be asked to trim [S17]. Treat that as submission-market risk, not a scientific norm. When a manuscript runs long, cuts should target duplicated function, not uniform percentages.

## Worldbuilding under pressure

The SFWA Turkey City Lexicon warns against lectures replacing specific dramatic evidence and against plot alternatives that stop action [S19]. John Brown’s SFWA story-cycle article notes that action/reaction units can be scene, sequence, summary, or omitted, and that reaction plausibly moves emotion → thought → decision [S20].

**Worldbuilding test:** every exposition block should do at least one now-job:

- alter route or tactic;
- clarify a threat;
- create wonder that changes attention;
- expose motive or value;
- sharpen a question;
- set up a later payoff.

If a block does only archive work, compress, move, or delete it.
