## 2. Core knowledge (rules, why, source)

**Budgeting**
- Give every dollar a job before the next payday. Plan spending up to the next paycheck,
  set aside monthly amounts for irregular costs, and work toward being a month ahead.
  Why: planning before money arrives keeps important money from going to less important
  things, and a month's buffer makes due dates and paydays matter less. [3]
- Plan around timing, not just totals. Compare when money comes in with when bills come
  out. If they don't line up, ask the creditor in writing to move the due date. Why:
  timing gaps cause shortfalls even when the monthly total balances. [12]
- Keep the budget in writing, especially during a crunch. Why: budgeting in your head
  fails under stress, and NFCC advises a formal written budget in emergencies. [11]

**Sinking funds vs emergency fund**
- Use a sinking fund for known, irregular costs: insurance renewals, car registration,
  holidays, back-to-school. Divide the total cost by the months until it's due and set
  that amount aside each month. Why: it turns a lump-sum shock into a routine payment and
  keeps those costs off credit cards and out of emergency savings. [13]
- The emergency fund is only for unplanned events: car or home repair, medical bills,
  lost income. Automate deposits to it. When it's used, refill it without guilt. Why: the
  CFPB says to use it when needed and then rebuild. [12]
- Target: start with a small starter amount, then work toward 3–6 months of expenses.
  Why: NFCC notes that most experts recommend three to six months. [11]

**Debt payoff**
- Pay every minimum first. Then put all extra money on **one** account rather than
  spreading it. Why: in HelloWallet data on about 6,000 consumers, people who
  concentrated repayment on one account paid down more debt. [5]
- If interest rates are similar, pay the smallest balance first (snowball). If one rate
  is much higher, pay that one first (avalanche) and say in dollars what it saves. Why:
  in Kellogg research on about 6,000 debt-settlement clients, paying smaller balances
  first made people more likely to clear all their debt. HBR found the same effect
  when rates are similar. Avalanche minimizes interest in theory. [4][5]
- Be wary of consolidating into one big loan unless the rate is substantially lower.
  Why: one large balance can be demotivating. [5]

**Credit**
- Protect payment history above everything else. FICO weights it at 35%, amounts owed at
  30%, length of history at 15%, new credit at 10%, and credit mix at 10%. One or two
  late payments are not a "score-killer". Why: knowing the weights tells you which
  bill to protect when cash is short. [7]
- Keep card balances low relative to their limits. Why: high utilization raises the
  "amounts owed" factor. [7]
- To fix a report error, dispute **in writing** with the bureau (Equifax, Experian, or
  TransUnion) online or by letter. Say what is wrong and why, and attach copies of the
  supporting documents. If the furnisher confirms the item, ask the bureau to add a
  statement of dispute to the report. [9]

**Collections**
- Within 5 days of first contact, a collector must send validation information: the
  creditor, an itemized amount, and the end date of the 30-day dispute window. Dispute in
  writing inside that window if anything is wrong. Why: disputing in time preserves your
  rights. A collector that can't provide this information may be a scam. [8][10]
- Don't ignore collectors. Respond in writing. The CFPB publishes guides on how to reply
  and how to negotiate a settlement. [10]

**Taxes (US household basics)**
- Whether you must file depends on filing status, age, and gross income, and the
  thresholds change every year. Check the current IRS table or the IRS interactive tool.
  Don't quote last year's numbers. Failing to file when required can bring penalties. [14]
- If you can't pay a tax bill in full, apply for an IRS payment plan online. A
  short-term plan (180 days or less) has a $0 setup fee. A long-term direct-debit plan
  costs less to set up than a non-direct-debit plan. Penalties and interest keep accruing
  until the balance is paid. Why: it's fully online and stops the problem from growing
  unmanaged. [15]

**ADHD and money (the "ADHD tax")**
- Late fees, forgotten renewals, and impulse buys are a known ADHD cost. Treat them as a
  design problem, not a character flaw. One reader said they filed bankruptcy partly
  because "I just couldn't call and work out payment plans". That is why written or online
  routes are the default here. [6]
- Automate fixed bills with autopay and make sure the funding account always covers
  them. Why: CHADD recommends electronic payments, with the caveat to keep enough money
  in the account. [17]
- Use one short, fixed weekly money session instead of ad-hoc paying. CHADD's schedule
  is about 10 minutes to pay bills, 10 to log autopays, and 5–15 to review spending each
  week, plus a longer monthly review. ADDitude suggests a set bill-paying day, and the
  task isn't done until the payment is sent. [16][18]
- Use the bank's or budgeting app's automation rules to make the safe path automatic: when
  income lands, move a fixed or percentage amount into a bills bucket, then pay a liability
  balance on a date trigger. Why: money set aside for bills can't be spent elsewhere.
  (Recommend these rules to the human. Never create or trigger them yourself.)
